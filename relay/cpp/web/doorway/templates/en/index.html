This is the reason why R2Я exists.

The big issue with all the existing messaging systems we have is that they belong to someone. There's a company behind them, so there's a person to go to. They're centralized. They control the server, and your information is saved on their server.

R2R shifts the paradigm and lets you save your data on your own storage, with each user having a small dedicated storage — and everything stored there is end-to-end encrypted and anonymized, so even the relay holding it can't read it or tie it to you. This way, there's no central place to control information, censor information, collect information, or delete information. And there's no one person to go to to stop the system.

What R2R is, in four bullets

Peer to peer

Every user runs (or borrows) their own relay — there's no central server that could be seized, subpoenaed, or shut down.

End-to-end encrypted

Every message and every attachment is encrypted between sender and recipient. Relays only ever handle ciphertext — and with onion routing, on by default, no relay sees both who sent a message and who it is for.

Chat · voice · video

1:1 and group text, mail with attachments, voice notes, video notes, live voice and video calls, screen share.

Invite-only, small circles

No search, no directory, no discovery. You exist to the people who invited you and the people you invite.

Two ways in

1. Get an invite code

  1. Someone gives you a codeA short token like R2R-XXXX-XXXX-XXXX. It can only be used once.
  2. You redeem it here{{HOST}} mints a fresh setup card — a QR + text string — that carries no identity, just a bootstrap secret.
  3. You import the card into the R2R appChoose a PIN or passphrase — a few random words is strongest. The card and your passphrase create your identity, and restore it on any other device later. Nobody else holds a copy.

2. Run your own relay — and hand out the invites yourself

If you have any Linux server — a home box, a $5 VPS — you can stand up a full peer in the network with one command. You are not dependent on anyone else's invite chain: your relay mints its own invite codes on first start, and as its owner you can mint as many as you like. The network doesn't have a centre; running a relay is how you claim your seat at it, and how it grows.

  1. Install itLog in to any recent x86-64 Linux server (a $5 VPS is enough) and paste four downloads and one command. No database, no web server, nothing else to install.
  2. Claim your own inviteYour relay writes five genesis codes on first start. Redeem one in the wallet: that is your identity, homed on your own box.
  3. Invite your friendsHand out the other four, or mint more from the wallet's owner panel or the shell. Each person you bring in gets three codes to pass on.

Frequently asked

I don't know anyone on R2R. How do I get in?

Run a relay. It is the one way in that needs nobody's permission: on first start your relay mints five invite codes, you claim one yourself and hand out the rest, and from then on you are the person people ask for an invite. It takes one command on any Linux server — see Run your own relay.

Why invite-only?

Every network scaled with search became a network where people are targeted, mass-messaged, or catalogued. R2R keeps the graph small on purpose — you exist only to the people you actually know.

Do you keep logs?

Relays keep only what they must, and only as ciphertext: messages waiting for you (deleted once your app confirms them, and after seven days at most), your own encrypted history, and voice or video files you send. They also know your identity’s fingerprint and which invite code brought you in. There is no who spoke to whom log to hand over, and no IP address is ever written down.

What if my inviter revokes me?

Root codes and their entire descendant tree can be revoked from the relay owner's admin panel. Once your identity exists it lives on your device and your relay — revoking your original code does not delete you, but it does prevent your unused invites from being redeemed.

Who runs {{HOST}}?

The operator of this specific relay runs this particular copy of the doorway — but this page is not special. What you're reading is one of potentially many identical copies: any relay operator can publish the same doorway on their own domain, and every copy points at that operator's own relay.

R2R itself has no owner. It is run by the community, and there is no single person, company, or country that can decide anything for the network or shut it down.

How can I reach a relay?

A relay can run under a domain name or with just an IP address. The wallet is a single HTML file you open on your own device, and it can talk to any relay. A copy opened from a website over https can only reach relays that have a real TLS certificate (wss://), so relays on a bare IP are used from the local file. Once connected, the experience is identical.

Where do invite codes come from?

Every relay operator can mint invite codes on their own relay, and there is no central issuer. A code minted on a relay makes that relay the newcomer’s first home. Members who join with a code in the app also receive three codes of their own. Those codes unlock once the member has really used the network: received a few messages, and chatted with the person who invited them, whose app then vouches for them. Every code remembers who issued it, so a relay owner can switch off a whole branch of invitations at once.
