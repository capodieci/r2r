What a relay knowsand what it can never know

Two lists, both honest. This exact honesty is the brand. If we ever quietly drop the “does hold” column, tell us.

No relay — and no one at all — ever collects

  • Phone numbers, email addresses, names, or any personal detail. There is no sign-up form anywhere in the network. An identity is an encryption keypair generated on your own device; your “address” is its fingerprint, a string of hex. Joining uses up an invite code: the relay remembers the code, when it was used, and the pseudonymous fingerprints of who issued it and who claimed it — never anything about the person.
  • Message contents. Payloads arrive already encrypted by the sender's device; nothing in the relay can decrypt them, and calls flow directly between devices.
  • IP addresses, in any durable form. Never written to the database, never written to the log, never retained after a socket closes. Connections appear in logs as a random id discarded on disconnect. (On {{HOST}} even the web server's access log is off.)
  • Who talks to whom. Every stored message travels through onion routing by default: it crosses three relays (five with “Extra privacy”), and no relay sees both ends. The first relay sees your device’s connection but not who the message is for; the middle ones see only other relays; the last one files it for the recipient but cannot tell who sent it.

What a relay does hold — said plainly

  • The recipient fingerprint of each stored payload (a dead drop must be filed under something), until the recipient’s app confirms it — seven days at most — and then securely erased.
  • Your own encrypted history (so a new device can restore it) and any voice or video files you send, as ciphertext it cannot open. History stays until the account is deleted; files expire like messages.
  • The invite tree: which fingerprint issued each code and which claimed it, and how many messages each member has received — that count is what unlocks a member’s own invite codes.
  • While you are connected: your IP address and which identity you proved, in memory only, gone the moment the connection closes.
  • A last-seen time on disk, rounded down to the hour. Live presence — online/away and precise last-seen — exists only in RAM, only for the contacts who watch you and the relay's owner, and is never persisted.
  • The public addresses of other relays, which are public infrastructure by design.
  • And, out of scope for any relay: an observer watching the wire itself can see message sizes and timing. Encryption hides content, not the fact that traffic exists, and someone able to watch both ends of an onion route could try to match them up by timing.
